SEO vs GEO

SEO Got You to the Top of Google. GEO Makes You the Answer in ChatGPT.

How AI actually decides who to recommend, and why ranking on page one is no longer the finish line.

By Narravue · AI-visibility & reputation

For about twenty years, the goal was simple and everyone agreed on it: rank on Google. Get to page one, ideally the top few links, and the clicks would come. An entire industry (search engine optimization, or SEO) grew up around gaming that one outcome. Keywords, backlinks, page speed, the whole liturgy. It worked because the game had one rule: be one of the links.

Then people stopped clicking the links.

Now they ask ChatGPT. They ask Perplexity. They read the AI summary Google puts at the top before the links even start. And the AI doesn't hand them ten options to choose from. It hands them an answer, with a few names already chosen. Which raises a genuinely new and slightly terrifying question for anyone who spent years climbing the Google rankings: what if you're on page one, and the AI still doesn't mention you?

Welcome to the difference between SEO and GEO.

What's the difference between SEO and GEO?

SEO (search engine optimization) is about ranking your page on a list of links, where a human still makes the final choice. GEO (generative engine optimization) is about being the name the AI includes inside its generated answer, where there is no list and often no click at all.

That's the whole shift in one sentence. SEO competed for a position. GEO competes for a mention. And a mention is much scarcer, because an AI answer names two or three sources where a search page listed ten.

You'll also hear this called "answer engine optimization" (AEO), which is roughly the same idea under a different label. The names are still settling. The reality underneath them is not: being found and being recommended by AI are now two separate jobs.

Why does being on page one not mean being in the answer?

Because the AI isn't reading the rankings. It's reading the content, and forming its own view.

When a search engine ranks pages, it's ordering documents for a human to pick from. When a generative engine answers a question, it's synthesizing an answer from what it has read, then deciding which sources are worth naming. Those are different mechanisms with different winners. A page can rank beautifully on Google and still never get cited by ChatGPT, because it was written to satisfy a ranking algorithm, not to be quoted by a machine.

Here's a way to feel the difference. Imagine two experts. One has a website that ranks well for their name. The other is mentioned consistently, in clear language, across many places the AI trusts, and describes what they do in plain, quotable sentences. Ask an AI who to hire, and it will tend to reach for the second one, because that's the one it can understand and cite confidently. Ranking got the first expert found. Being citable got the second one recommended.

So how does AI actually decide who to recommend?

Nobody outside the labs has the exact recipe, and it shifts. But across the major engines, a few ingredients reliably matter:

Consistency of identity. The same clear story about who you are and what you do, repeated across many sources. When the web says one coherent thing about you, the model states it confidently. When the signals conflict, or you're confused with a similarly named company, it hedges or guesses. (Ask me how I know. A machine once confidently told the world our own firm was something we're not.)

Corroboration from sources it trusts. One claim on your own website is weak evidence. The same thing echoed across credible, independent places is strong evidence. AI weighs who else says it, not just what you say about yourself.

Clarity a machine can lift. Content written in plain, structured, factual language (clear questions, direct answers, unambiguous statements of who does what) is easy for a model to quote. Clever, meandering copy that a human enjoys is often useless to a machine trying to extract a fact. Write things a model can copy into a sentence.

Structured signals. Things like clean page structure and schema markup (the invisible labels that tell a machine "this is an organization, this is what it does") help engines read you accurately rather than guessing.

Presence and recency. Simply being there, clearly and often, and keeping it current. Visibility compounds, and stale or thin footprints get overlooked.

Notice the theme: none of this is about being loudest, and none of it is about being best at your actual craft. It's about being legible to a machine and corroborated by the web. Being genuinely good is necessary for keeping clients. It is not, by itself, what gets you named by an AI.

Does SEO still matter, then?

Yes, and anyone telling you SEO is dead is overselling. GEO doesn't replace SEO; it sits on top of it.

Here's why they're linked: the generative engines often read the same web that search engines index. If Google can't find or make sense of you, ChatGPT probably can't either. Good SEO fundamentals (a findable, well-structured, fast, clearly-written site) are the foundation GEO is built on. What's changed is that reaching the top of the rankings is no longer the finish line. It's the starting line for the new race: getting cited in the answer.

So the honest framing is: keep the SEO basics, and add the GEO layer on top. One gets you found. The other gets you recommended.

How do you actually become the answer?

The practical work follows straight from the ingredients above:

Tell one consistent story about yourself everywhere, in the same words, so the machines stop guessing and start quoting. Get that story corroborated by sources beyond your own website. Write your key content in clear, structured, quotable language, with the facts stated plainly. Make sure your site is technically legible to machines. And keep it current, because recency counts.

Then, crucially, check. Ask the AI engines what they say about you and your field, and treat the answer as data. It tells you exactly where the gaps and the wrong guesses are, which tells you what to fix next.

If you want the one-line version of this whole article: SEO was about climbing a list of links. GEO is about becoming the sentence. The companies and people who understand that shift early get to be the name the machines recommend, while everyone still optimizing for page one wonders why the clicks stopped coming.

The answer is being written right now, by a machine, for someone who's deciding whether to trust you. The only question is whether your name is in it.
